Behind the Proposal

What Planning With Me Looks Like

One Greece trip, from the first email to the finished itinerary — and everything I send in between. Yours would look nothing like it.

By Keith Pence · Travel Advisor

People usually come to me with a version of the same sentence: we want to go to Greece, we have about two weeks, and we don't know where to start. What follows is what happens next — using one recent trip as the example, with the names and dates changed.

Nora and Adam were planning an anniversary trip for the following October: ten nights in the Cyclades, flying long-haul from the West Coast, with a number in mind for hotels and no sense of how to spend it across four islands. They'd been sent a photo of a hotel on Folegandros by a friend and weren't sure it was reachable.

What stays the same from trip to trip is the process below. Nothing else does. I don't keep a set of Greece itineraries and fit people into whichever one is closest — every plan gets built from the answers you give me, and I've never sent the same one twice. Which islands you end up on is a decision we make together, out of what you tell me you actually want from the trip.

Here's what I did with that.

Stage One

I start with the flights, not the islands

Almost every Greece itinerary I'm shown by a prospective client has the same flaw: it was planned on a map. The islands look close together, so the route seems obvious — and then the flights don't exist, or they exist twice a week, and the whole thing has to be rebuilt.

So the first thing I do is find out what's actually flying on your dates. For Nora and Adam that meant two viable one-stop routings — into Santorini via Munich, or into Mykonos via London — and one that looked good on paper and wasn't: the island they most wanted to swim on needed two stops and a late arrival, which makes it a ferry leg in the middle of the trip rather than a place to land.

October is a thinning schedule in Greece. Knowing which routes survive into the second week of the month is what decides the shape of everything after it.

The route isn't a preference. It's whatever the flight schedule will actually support, arranged in the nicest possible order.

Stage Two

You get options, not one answer

This is the part that surprises people. I don't send back a single itinerary and ask what you think. I send several complete ways through the trip, each one built around a real arrival and a real departure, and each one with a different personality.

Nora and Adam got five. The design-forward one, built around the cliff hotel on Folegandros their friend had sent them. The most relaxing one, four nights on the swimming island. The best-food one. And two three-island routes that trade a couple of slower mornings for the full range — glamour, then village, then caldera.

Those five existed because of what they'd told me — an anniversary, a long flight they didn't want to repeat on the far end, one hotel they were already in love with, and a strong preference for swimming over sightseeing. Someone travelling with kids in July, or with four nights instead of ten, gets a completely different five. That's the point of doing it this way: the options are drawn from your answers, not picked off a list of routes I already had.

Each came with the nights split island by island, the ferry legs and their real durations, and one line on who that version is for. They picked in a day, because the choice was between five clear characters rather than a hundred hotels — and then we adjusted the one they chose until it was theirs.

Stage Four

The budget, weighted rather than spread

Nora and Adam had a fixed number for hotels and the instinct most people have, which is to divide it evenly by the number of nights. That's usually the wrong move, and it was here.

Islands don't reward the same nightly rate. On some of them the gap between a good hotel and the right one is the trip; on others that same money buys a slightly better view and nothing you'd remember.

How I split it
Spend up here. Santorini and Folegandros, where the best properties are a genuinely different experience from the tier below — the caldera suite and the cliff hotel are the nights they'll still be describing to people a year later.
Hold back here. Paros. The strongest hotels on the island sit well under caldera pricing, and the days are about the town and the water rather than the room you booked.
Move it off the room entirely here. Milos, where the difference between two good hotels is small and a private boat day around the south coast is the thing worth paying for instead.

Same total, weighted so the expensive nights are the ones earning it — and the rest of it spent on days rather than rooms.

Stage Five

The logistics, handled rather than listed

This is the least glamorous stage and the one that decides whether the trip feels effortless.

Ferries between the Cyclades go on sale in batches, and the good crossings on the good dates sell out early — so they get booked the day the schedules open, not the month before you travel. Private port transfers on both ends of every crossing, because a ferry arrival with luggage and no plan is where a good day comes apart. And a hard rule I don't bend: never a ferry on the same day as a flight. Cycladic boats cancel for wind with a few hours' notice, and a cancelled ferry on a flight day is a missed flight rather than an inconvenience.

Where the sea isn't the right answer at all, there's a helicopter — Athens to Santorini in under an hour through my partnership with Hoper, or a private boat directly between two islands whose public ferry schedule doesn't suit the day.

Stage Six

Then it becomes an itinerary

Once the route is chosen and the hotels are confirmed, the proposal turns into the thing you actually travel with: every night, every transfer, every reservation and timing in one document, delivered about a week before you leave once everything is locked.

Dinner reservations at the tables that need booking weeks ahead. The guide meeting you at the Acropolis at the right hour rather than the crowded one. The boat charter that leaves before the day-trip fleet. All of it confirmed and timed around the rest of the day before you get on the plane.

What I need from you to start

Less than you'd think. Your dates or the window you're working within, who's travelling, roughly what you want the trip to feel like, and an honest budget range. That last one matters more than people expect — it's what decides where the money goes rather than whether I'll take the trip on.

Beyond that, the more you tell me about how you actually like to travel — slow mornings or early starts, a boat every day, dinner as the main event — the more the plan comes back looking like you rather than like a generic Greece trip.

What I don't need is a finished itinerary. If you already have one, bring it and I'll tell you what's wrong with it. That's usually the fastest first conversation we can have.
