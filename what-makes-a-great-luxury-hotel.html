What actually distinguishes a great luxury hotel?

It isn’t the marble. After touring and staying in hotels from the Cyclades to the Dolomites, these are the things I’ve learned to look for.

By Keith Pence · November 2026

When excellence is your day job, marble lobbies stop impressing you.

Before I was a travel advisor I spent ten years in wine and spirits, building luxury brands. I stayed at some of the nicest hotels in the world because that’s where the business happened. Now I’m a Fora X advisor, and my job is to tour hotels, sleep in the rooms where it matters, and keep notes. No press trips, no sponsored positions.

Do that long enough and you start noticing what actually separates a great property: the service that anticipates, the room that’s sited right, the details that hold up on a second and third visit. Almost none of it shows up in the photos. Here is what I look for.

The short answer

Eight things, in order

01
Service that anticipates. They do it before you ask, and they remember you.
02
Someone in charge. An owner, a family or a general manager who is actually there.

The long answer

What I look for

01Service that anticipates, and remembers

At Hotel Eden in Rome, I got the fastest coffee I’ve ever had delivered. It sounds small, but it tells you everything about how that team runs. The great hotels do things before you ask. The very best remember you, too.

I’ve been back to Montage Healdsburg more times than any other hotel on my list, including for my 31st birthday. There’s one masseuse I make a point of seeing every time. The returning-guest experience is real, and it’s why I keep going back. At Parīlio on Paros, what I loved most was that the staff actually cared to know your preferences by name.

02Someone is actually in charge

My favorite example is Hotel Santa Caterina in Amalfi, run by the same family since 1904. When I mentioned a client of mine who had stayed, Signora Giusi remembered them right away, down to the room numbers. Reception still keeps the calendar and notes in real books with real pens. In a world of constant tech, that face-to-face connection really means something.

You see it in Greece too. Bill & Coo is independently owned, with staff who return season after season. To me, that’s one of the best signs that things work well behind the scenes. At Gundari, when my clients flag anything at all, the team responds with genuine gratitude and speed. That’s the ownership mentality you hope for at this level.

Signora Giusi remembered them right away, down to the room numbers.Hotel Santa Caterina, Amalfi

03A scale where they know you

Size isn’t everything, but it matters. Aman Venice has twenty-four rooms, which means the team knows you. Portrait Firenze has 37, and it’s simply a place where you feel valued. Cheval Blanc Paris has seventy-two keys in a city where the grand palaces run three hundred, so the public spaces never feel public.

Scale isn’t everything, though. Leadership is. At Forestis, service was warm, anticipatory and flawless across every interaction, right up to the general manager, Günther, taking time to chat.

04The room matters more than the hotel

This is the thing I most wish more travelers knew. At Le Sirenuse, I would only stay in a true sea-view room or suite; the rest look onto a courtyard, or sit right on top of the pool, and it’s loud. At Montage Healdsburg, the vineyard rooms are the magic. In any Italian palazzo, I want the piano nobile, the noble floor one level up, where the ceilings are much higher. At Santa Caterina, ask for an upper floor. I stayed in room 45, and I can request it for you.

That’s why my reviews name the room I’d book. Two people can stay at the same five-star hotel and have completely different trips.

05Quiet you can come home to

In Rome, you step out the door and it’s busy and crowded. Hotel Eden sits on a quiet residential street, so you’re close to everything and still come home to calm. At Il Sereno, you can only get in if you’re staying or arrive by boat, so there are almost no day visitors. That’s the point.

At Canaves Epitome, my #1 in Greece and perhaps one of the top three stays of my life, what it buys you is space and serenity. That’s much of what you’re paying for at this level.

06A sense of place

A great hotel feels like where it is. NOS on Sifnos feels deeply connected to the island rather than separate from it; nothing is excess, everything is intentional. Borgo San Felice has been restored so carefully that it never feels like a set. Within a day, the borgo feels like your village.

I don’t usually love modern hotels in Italy, because so many could be anywhere. Il Sereno is the exception, done exactly right.

07Details that hold up

The sheets at Il Sereno are the softest I’ve ever slept on. It sounds small; it isn’t. The best breakfasts I had in Greece were at Bill & Coo. I was usually full until dinner. At Borgo Pignano, the team got me into my room within minutes of a very late arrival, with a plate of charcuterie, wine and water waiting.

It also means judgment about what not to build. I’m not a fan of pools floating on a lake: they rock with the water and can make you queasy. Il Sereno built its pool on land, so it never rocks. And on a remote island like Folegandros, a spa and gym that punch far above the room count tell you the owners thought it through.

08Honesty about who it’s not for

Every great hotel is wrong for someone. Il San Pietro is in my Italy top 10, and I’d still tell you to skip it with young kids: it’s a formal, grown-up house. That’s why every “right for” I write gets a “wrong for.” A ranking that can’t tell you who shouldn’t book is really just an ad.

And some famous hotels don’t make the cut. I had a pretty disappointing stay at Le Sirenuse. At Palazzo Avino, the vibe felt a bit awkward and I never fully settled in. Even the hotels I love most aren’t perfect: twenty stays in at Montage, I notice a few more little misses lately than in year one. The team always makes it right, and that’s the real test.

What doesn’t matter as much

Things that impress less than you’d think

  • The lobby. Marble, chandeliers and a big atrium make a first impression. The service that anticipates makes the stay.
  • The brand. I’ve had extraordinary stays at independent and family-run hotels, and awkward ones at famous names.
  • The price. On some islands, the gap between a good hotel and the right one is the whole trip. On others, the same money buys a slightly better view and nothing you’d remember. Hotel Eden is usually priced slightly below its Rome competitors and is, I think, a better product. And Gundari, at $600, was the best value on my whole Greece list.
  • The arrival as theater. A grand entrance is lovely. What matters is whether, when you step off the boat, someone says your name, and the hard part is over before it starts.

Before you ask

The questions I get

What makes a luxury hotel great rather than just expensive?

Service that anticipates what you need and remembers you, someone in charge who is actually present, the right room, quiet, a sense of place and details that hold up on a second visit. Very little of that shows up in the photos.

Are small hotels better than big ones?

Often, because the team gets to know you: Aman Venice has 24 rooms and Portrait Firenze 37. But a larger hotel can be excellent when its leadership is present, and a bigger hotel can mean better upgrade odds.

Does the room I book really matter?

Enormously. At Le Sirenuse, I’d only stay in a true sea-view room. At Montage Healdsburg, the vineyard rooms are the magic. In an Italian palazzo, ask for the piano nobile. That’s why every review on this site names the room I’d book.

Are family-run hotels better than big brands?

Not always, but many of my favorites are owner-run, including Hotel Santa Caterina, Grand Hotel Tremezzo and Bill & Coo. Staff who return season after season is one of the best signs a hotel works well behind the scenes.

How do you review hotels?

I tour the properties and sleep in the rooms where it matters. A hotel only gets ranked after I’ve toured it or slept in it. No press trips and no sponsored positions, and every “right for” gets a “wrong for.”

Does booking through an advisor change the stay?

It can. You pay the same rate as booking direct, and at partner hotels you typically get a space-available upgrade, daily breakfast and a hotel credit. Just as important, the hotel hears from me before you arrive.
