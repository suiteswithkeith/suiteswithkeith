The Greek Island Matrix

Fourteen islands, placed honestly. Twenty-one hotels I'd actually book. No rankings, no hidden gems that turn out to be mobbed.

By Keith Pence · Travel Advisor

Every Greek island list I read is a ranking, and rankings are useless when the question is which island suits you. Santorini isn't better than Serifos. They're answers to different questions.

So I built a map instead of a list. Two axes, fourteen islands, each one placed where it actually sits rather than where the marketing puts it. Then the part that matters: the twenty-one hotels I'd put a client in, and who each one is for.

If you came here from the carousel, this is the full version — every hotel, with my notes. If you're planning a honeymoon specifically, I mapped out a fourteen-night route through five of these islands with private transfers instead of ferries.

And once you've found your island, the next question is which one goes with it. I wrote that up separately: five pairings that make sense, each built around the ferries and flights that actually exist.

How to read it

The two axes

Rustic to polished is about the island's infrastructure and its expectations of you. Rustic doesn't mean rough — it means a taverna instead of a tasting menu, and a rental car instead of a transfer.

Undiscovered to well-trodden is about crowds in August, not about whether travel writers have found it. Sifnos is written about constantly and still feels calm. Santorini is neither.

A filled dot means I have a hotel there I'd stake my name on. Those are the nine islands below.
A hollow ring means I know and love the island but I'd send you for the place, not the property. Koufonisia and Amorgos are that group — and Kimolos was too, until this summer. See the bonus at the end of the list.

Where They Actually Sit

The matrix tells you what an island feels like. This tells you where it is — which matters, because a route is only as good as its ferry connections. Everything above the Crete line is the Cyclades; most pairs of neighbours here are under two hours apart by boat.

Not to scale, and not trying to be — the point is who your neighbours are. Dashed lines are the connections I actually use when building routes.

The Polished End

Santorini · Mykonos · Paros · Crete

These four have the hotels, the restaurants, and the crowds. That's not a criticism — sometimes you want a proper concierge and a sommelier. The skill here is choosing the property that gives you the setting without the scrum.

A note on the prices: every “from” figure is an approximate September starting rate for the hotel's entry-level room, pulled while writing this. Rates move daily and none of these include the upgrade, breakfast, or credit I add when I book — so treat them as calibration, and ask me for exact dates.

Canaves Oia Suites
From ~$1,000 · Sept
Oia caldera · 46 suites & villas · Ages 13+

Oia at its most composed. The brand started in the family's 17th-century wine caves in 1985, and the 2023 expansion kept the standard: suites cut into the cliff properly, not decoratively, each with a private plunge pool, a spa built inside a natural cave, and Petra's eight-course tasting menu over the water. The service is the most consistent on the island.

BOOK IT IFyou want the caldera view and you want it done without fuss.

From ~$900 · Sept
Oia caldera · 24 suites · Ages 13+

The view everyone came to Santorini for, from a hotel small enough that you're not queuing for a sun lounger. Twenty-four cave-carved suites, every one with an outdoor hot tub or plunge pool facing the volcano — and dinner at Lauda, Oia's oldest restaurant, with a menu shaped alongside three-Michelin-star chef Emmanuel Renaut. Canaves Epitome
From ~$800 · Sept
Above Ammoudi Bay, Oia · 53 suites & villas · Family-friendly

Not on the caldera's cliff path, and better for it — Epitome sits on Oia's northern edge above the little fishing harbour of Ammoudi, with resort-style grounds the cave hotels can't offer: bigger rooms, private heated pools, an actual garden, two restaurants, and a rooftop pool the hotel claims as the island's largest. It's also the one Canaves property that genuinely welcomes children.

Where I send most people first. Paros has Mykonos's good looks, better food, and about a third of the attitude.

Parìlio
From ~$550 · Sept
Near Naoussa · 46 suites & villas · Design Hotels

Arched, minimalist, deeply designed — the kind of hotel that photographs well and still works when you're actually living in it. The cruciform pool is the most copied image on Paros, and a 2025 expansion added new suites and villas plus Parōn, the restaurant now run by chef Thanos Feskos of two-Michelin-star pedigree. Families are welcome — there's even a proper kids' program. My most-booked on the island.

Naoussa beachfront · 40 suites · Luxury Collection

Sophisticated, genuinely beachfront on Agii Anargyri, and a ten-minute walk along the coast to Naoussa's harbour — the location the whole island wishes it had. All forty suites sit around a half-moon infinity pool that mirrors the bay, with a private beach club below and the Elios Spa at the resort's highest point. Smaller and looser than Parìlio, with a younger room.

Crete

Crete isn't Cyclades — it's its own island group, and larger than all the Cyclades combined. I include it because it solves a problem the small islands can't: it has enough going on for two weeks without a boat.

Acro Suites
From ~$550 · Sept
Agia Pelagia · 49 suites & villas · Adults only

Cliff-edge on the north coast, half an hour from Heraklion's airport, built down the rock face in tiers above Mononaftis Bay. Every one of the 49 suites and villas has a private pool, the cave suites come with heated grotto pools, and there are three real restaurants — modern Greek, Nikkei, and Cretan in the olive groves — plus a hammam-and-cold-plunge Bath House. Adults only, and priced well under the caldera.

BOOK IT IFyou want Santorini drama at Crete value — without children in the next pool.

Minos Beach Art Hotel
From ~$650 · Sept
Agios Nikolaos · 125 bungalows · Since 1963

Bungalows on the water in Agios Nikolaos, none more than about twenty steps from the sea, with more than 55 commissioned sculptures scattered through the gardens — an open-air museum run by the founding family since 1963. La Bouillabaisse has held a Greek Cuisine Award since 2020, town is walkable, and children of all ages are genuinely welcome. Older in the best sense — established, shaded, unhurried.

BOOK IT IFyou want a garden, art, and a sea you can walk into from your room — kids included.

The Middle

Milos · Sifnos · Folegandros

This is where I spend most of my own time. Real islands with real hotels — enough infrastructure to be comfortable, not enough to be crowded. If you only trust one recommendation from this page, make it this group.

Milos

Volcanic rock and impossible water. Milos looks like nowhere else in Greece, and it's best seen from a boat.

Skinopi Lodge
From ~$500 · Sept
Bay of Milos · 7 villas · No pool, no restaurant — on purpose

Seven stone villas on the cliff above a tiny fishing hamlet, and nothing else. No restaurant, no pool, no reception desk to speak of — just volcanic-stone architecture (the design press noticed), outdoor kitchens, a private sea deck, and a breakfast basket delivered to your terrace. The point is the island, and the lodge refuses to compete with it.

Two restaurants and the Elios Spa's outdoor heated pool round it out. Dark stone, pale sea, minimal on purpose.

Verina Astra

The Quiet End

Serifos · Tinos

Two islands most people skip, each with exactly one hotel I'd send you to. That's not a shortage — it's the whole character of the place.

Serifos

Perma
From ~$300 · Sept
Lia cove · 5 residences · Adults only

Five stone residences carved into the slope above a secluded cove at Lia, by MOLD Architects — the design press got here before the travel press did. Each has a private pool and a full outdoor kitchen; there's no restaurant, no bar, no scene, just a yoga shala, sound baths, and a Chora up the hill that climbs like something out of a different century. Serifos rewards people who don't need a scene, and Perma is built for exactly them.

Odera

Islands I Know and Love

Kimolos · Koufonisia · Naxos · Syros · Amorgos

Five islands on the matrix with no hotel pick, and that's an honest answer rather than a gap. What I'd do instead: pair them. Koufonisia by boat from Naxos. Amorgos for a few nights in a well-chosen rental if you're the kind of traveller who wants that. Naxos and Syros both have good hotels — just not ones I'd single out over the twenty-one above.

And then there's Kimolos — one of my favourite places in Greece. A fishing harbour, a handful of tavernas, water you won't believe. For years my honest answer was that there was no hotel there I'd stake my name on. That answer just changed, which is why the list stays at twenty-one and this page gets a bonus.

Espera Suites

Kalamitsi, Kimolos · 18 suites · The island’s first five-star · Opened 2025

The bonus, because it broke my own rule. Espera opened in 2025 as the first five-star Kimolos has ever had: eighteen suites in the quiet Kalamitsi corner of the island, a short walk from two of its best beaches, with the top categories carrying private pools and a restaurant — Amoni, under chef Dimitris Dimitriadis — that's worth the boat ride on its own. The design borrows from the island's Kastro: sloped walls, courtyards, a sheltered-village feel. Kimolos keeps its hollow ring on the chart. This is the asterisk.

And if a hotel isn't the point at all: I'm happy to find you a boat house through my trusted partners — like the one I stayed at on Kimolos, with the water a few steps from the bed. It's the version of Greece most people don't believe still exists.

Who Goes Where

The matrix places the islands; this places you. Most mismatched trips I untangle come down to the same mistake — a couple sent to a family island, or a family sent to a couples' island — so here's the honest sorting.

Honeymoons & couples

SantoriniFolegandrosMilosSifnos

Santorini earns its reputation for two or three nights in a caldera suite — Canaves Oia or Andronis — then move on before the crowds wear you down. The rest of the honeymoon belongs in the middle of the matrix: Gundari on Folegandros is the single most romantic setting in the Cyclades, and Skinopi or Erema on Milos give you the kind of privacy resorts advertise and rarely deliver. If you want it fully adults-only, that's Bill & Coo on Mykonos, and on Crete both Acro Suites and Minos Palace. The full route is in the honeymoon guide.

Families

CreteParosNaxos

Crete is the easy answer: direct flights, real distances, and hotels with room to spread out — Minos Beach Art Hotel takes children of all ages, and the walk-into-the-sea bungalows and sculpture gardens make it the rare hotel that works for three generations at once. (Note that Acro Suites and Minos Palace, my other two Crete picks, are both adults-only.) In the Cyclades, Paros is the family island that doesn't feel like a compromise — Cosme puts you a stroll from Naoussa's harbour, and Cali on Mykonos is the one polished-end hotel I'd genuinely hand a multi-generation group. Naxos has the best beaches for children in the whole chain; I pair it with Paros rather than base a trip on it. What I don't do is put families on the caldera — cliff paths, steps, and strollers are a bad mix.

Friends & groups

MykonosParos

Mykonos is still the best island in Greece for a group that wants restaurants, beach clubs, and a scene — the trick is sleeping somewhere calm, which is exactly what Kalesma and Cali are for. Paros does the same trip at two-thirds the price and half the attitude, with Naoussa as the night out.

Second-timers & the quiet-seeking

SerifosTinosSifnosKoufonisiaAmorgosKimolos

If you've done the famous islands and want the Greece you suspected was hiding behind them, this is the group. Sifnos for the food, Tinos for the villages and walking, Serifos for beaches with nobody on them. One hotel each — Stamna or Verina Astra, Odera, Perma — and that scarcity is the point.

Then there are the three hollow rings on the map: Koufonisia, Amorgos, and Kimolos. These are second-timer islands in the purest sense — you go for the place, not the property. Koufonisia has the best swimming water in the Cyclades, Amorgos has the monastery and the hiking, and Kimolos is the fishing-harbour Greece most people think no longer exists — and as of this summer it finally has a hotel worth the name (see the bonus above). I build these islands in as day trips, a few well-chosen rental nights, or now a proper stay on Kimolos, and they're often the part of the trip clients talk about most.

How to Book the Best Hotels

Everything above is useless if the room is gone, so here is how the booking side actually works.

Book the small islands first. Gundari, Verina Astra, Skinopi — these are hotels with seven to thirty rooms, and the good categories for June and September are gone by February. Santorini and Mykonos have depth; they're the last pieces you place, not the first.

Book the room category, not the hotel. The gap between a hotel's entry room and the room it's famous for is usually the whole experience — a caldera view at Canaves, a private pool at Epitome, the sunset side at Kalesma. If the budget forces a choice, take the better room at a cheaper hotel over the worst room at a famous one. Every time.

Six to nine months out is the window. For a June or September trip, that means booking in the autumn or over the holidays. Later than that isn't hopeless — but it means building the route around what's left rather than what's right.

Use an advisor — and not only because I am one. I book these properties through direct relationships and programs like Virtuoso, which means the same rate you'd pay booking direct usually comes with an upgrade at check-in, daily breakfast, a property credit, and someone the hotel answers to if anything goes sideways. It costs you nothing; the hotel pays me. The only scenario where booking direct wins is a flash sale with harsh prepayment terms — and I'll tell you when that's the better deal.

When Everything Is Actually Open

The islands are not a year-round destination, and the closing dates are not a formality — they decide trips. Every hotel on this page shuts for the winter, and as a rule, the smaller and quieter the hotel, the shorter its season: the caldera giants run April to the end of October, while Stamna is done by mid-October and Cali closes before almost anyone else. If you're eyeing a late-season trip, check the right-hand edge of this chart before you fall in love with a hotel.

Approximate windows — hotels shift their exact dates each year, so confirm with me before you book flights. The gold bands are the two windows I'd aim for.

Two things the chart makes obvious. First, the sweet spots — late May to mid-June, and September — sit comfortably inside every hotel's season, which is one more reason they're the right answer. Second, October is a real gamble: half this list is closed or closing, ferries thin out, and the islands that reward spontaneity stop offering any.

Planning notes

Two islands per week is the right pace. Three is possible; four means you spend the trip in transit.

Book Sifnos and Folegandros first — the good rooms there are limited and they go early. Santorini and Mykonos have depth, so they can wait.

Private transfers between islands — chartered boats, or helicopters on the routes that have them — cost more than ferries but change the trip completely: no 6am port queues, no schedule built around a boat. On a two-week route it's typically $5,000–$7,000 for the difference.

Late May to mid-June and September are the windows. July and August are hot and full; October is a gamble on the weather but the islands are yours.

Common Questions

Which island should I start with if it's my first time?

Paros. It has the best balance of food, beaches, hotels, and ease, and it connects easily to almost everything else on the matrix.

Is Santorini worth it?

Yes, for two or three nights, in the right hotel, outside July and August. The caldera is genuinely one of the great sights in the Mediterranean. The mistake is staying a week.

How many islands in two weeks?

Three, comfortably. Four if the transfers are private and you're willing to move at pace. Five is a specific kind of trip and needs to be built that way from the start.

Do I need to rent a car?

On Milos, Serifos, Tinos, Naxos and Crete, yes. On Folegandros, Sifnos and Santorini you can manage without. On Mykonos and Paros it depends entirely on the hotel.
